How SmileBERTa predicts fragments

From a SMILES string to a validated, binding-relevant fragment: the chemistry, the transformer, and every step of the inference pipeline.

Why fragments?

Fragment-based drug discovery (FBDD) starts small. Instead of screening millions of large, drug-sized molecules, chemists look for small molecular fragments, usually under 300 Da, that bind weakly but efficiently to a "hot spot" on the target protein. Those fragments are then grown, linked or merged into potent leads. Several approved drugs began this way, including vemurafenib and venetoclax.

Fragments work because they sample chemical space efficiently. A small fragment that binds at all usually makes high-quality interactions, measured as ligand efficiency: binding energy per heavy atom. SmileBERTa's fragment model learns which part of a larger drug-like molecule is the fragment worth keeping.

The Rule of Three

Good fragments usually satisfy the Rule of Three (Congreve et al., 2003), the fragment counterpart of Lipinski's Rule of Five. SmileBERTa checks every predicted fragment against it:

PropertyRule of ThreeWhy it matters
Molecular weight≤ 300 DaLeaves room to grow into a drug-sized lead
cLogP≤ 3Keeps the fragment soluble at screening concentrations
H-bond donors / acceptors≤ 3 eachLimits desolvation penalties
Rotatable bonds≤ 3Rigid fragments lose less entropy on binding
Polar surface area≤ 60 Å²Supports permeability once elaborated

Molecules as text: SMILES

A SMILES string (Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System) writes a molecule as a line of characters. Atoms are element symbols (lower-case means aromatic), branches go in parentheses, = and # mark double and triple bonds, and matching digits close rings. Because a molecule becomes a sentence, methods from natural-language processing can be applied directly to chemistry.

Tokenization

Before the model sees a molecule, the SMILES string is split into tokens using a byte-pair encoding (BPE) vocabulary of 767 tokens learned from chemical data. Frequent substrings such as CC, (= or ccccc become single tokens, so the model works with meaningful chemical "words" rather than individual characters. The transformer

SmileBERTa's models start from ChemBERTa (seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1), a RoBERTa encoder pre-trained on molecules from the ZINC database with masked-language modelling. Pre-training teaches the model SMILES grammar and common chemical patterns before it ever sees a fragment task. The fragment model is then fine-tuned from that checkpoint.

What self-attention captures

Self-attention lets every token look at every other token and decide how much each one matters. In SMILES this is essential: a ring-closure digit at the end of a string refers back to an atom many characters earlier, and whether a group is part of a ring changes what it means chemically. Each of the 12 heads can specialise in a different relationship, such as bond partners, ring membership or nearby polar groups.

Fine-tuning for fragments

Fragment training data in the SmileBERTa project pairs a drug molecule with its fragments. The fragments come from RDKit's BRICS rules, which cut molecules at chemically sensible, synthetically accessible bonds. During fine-tuning the encoder learns to emit, position by position, the tokens of the fragment associated with the input drug. The masked-language-model head that ChemBERTa used during pre-training is reused as the output layer.

loss = − Σpositions t log pθ( fragment_tokent | drug_tokens1..128 )

Because the model rewrites the whole sequence at once rather than generating one token at a time, inference is a single forward pass. That makes it fast, but the output is not guaranteed to be a valid molecule, which is why the validation steps below exist.

The inference pipeline

Here is exactly what happens when you press Predict fragment:

  1. Validate the input with RDKit so malformed SMILES are rejected before reaching the model.
  2. Tokenize and encode. The cached model runs one forward pass, typically well under a second on CPU.
  3. Decode the highest-scoring token at each position and join them into a candidate SMILES string.
  4. Validate and repair. Valid outputs are used directly. Invalid outputs go to a dedicated SMILES-corrector service, and if that fails, the closest of 25,364 reference fragments (by normalised edit distance) is used instead.
  5. Canonicalize, embed in 3D (ETKDG v3 + MMFF94), and compute properties: MW, cLogP, HBD/HBA, TPSA, rotatable bonds, QED, InChIKey.

Reading your results

  • Highlighted atoms in the input structure show where the fragment occurs in the parent molecule, when it is an exact substructure.
  • “Direct model output” means the transformer's answer was valid as generated. “Corrected output” means it was repaired, so treat it as a suggestion rather than a strong prediction.
  • The Rule-of-Three badges tell you whether the fragment is a good starting point for growing or linking.
Limitations. The target selector (KRAS, mTOR, AKT1, ERK2) is recorded with each run, but the current public checkpoint is a single shared fragment model, so predictions do not yet change with the target. The model has no 3D information about the protein. Validate promising fragments with docking and, ultimately, experiments.
