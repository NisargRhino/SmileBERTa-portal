What it is

A drug-discovery workbench that runs in a browser

SmileBERTa combines transformer language models trained on chemistry, physics-based molecular modelling and interactive 3D visualization in one workspace, so anyone can go from a molecule written as text to a docked lead candidate without installing software. Every tool is free and works without an account; an account saves your work permanently.

Who it is for

  • Students and educators in medicinal chemistry, biochemistry or computational biology. Every tool has an illustrated guide explaining the science.
  • Researchers who want fast hypotheses on fragments, therapeutic class, binding poses and developability.
  • Anyone curious about how AI is changing drug discovery.

What makes it different

  • AI and physics together. Model predictions are checked with RDKit, force fields and AutoDock Vina.
  • One connected workflow. Results hand off between tools: classify, fragment, link, build, dock, profile.
  • Every number is traceable. Each computed value carries the equation, rule or paper it came from.
  • Nothing to install. It all runs in the browser.

AI model

Fragment Prediction /fragmentation

Enter a drug-like molecule and SmileBERTa predicts the fragment most relevant to binding, then validates it chemically and scores it against the fragment-likeness Rule of Three. Fragment-based drug discovery starts from small, efficient binders and grows them into leads; this tool proposes the starting point. It is the platform's own model, trained for this task.

A real prediction. The β-blocker scaffold CC(C)(C)NCC(O)COc1ccc(O)c2c1CCC(=O)N2 (308.4 Da) gives the fragment CC1=CC=C(O)C12CCC(=O)N2 (165.2 Da, cLogP 1.04) — valid as generated, and passing the Rule of Three.

How to use it

  1. Paste a SMILES string or pick an example (oxazolidinones, a β-blocker, a peptidomimetic, imatinib).
  2. Choose a target (KRAS, mTOR, AKT1, ERK2) and click Predict fragment.
  3. Compare input and fragment in 2D or 3D. Highlighted atoms show where the fragment sits in the parent.
  4. Add it to your fragment library, save it, or send it to Docking, ADMET or the Builder.

Batch mode runs up to 50 molecules from a CSV and exports the results.

Under the hood

  • The SMILES is split into tokens from a 767-token chemical vocabulary and passed through a 6-layer RoBERTa transformer (ChemBERTa-initialised).
  • The model writes out the fragment's tokens in a single forward pass.
  • If the output is not a valid molecule, it is repaired by a SMILES-corrector model, or failing that snapped to the closest of 25,364 reference fragments.
  • RDKit canonicalizes the fragment, builds a 3D conformer (ETKDG + MMFF94) and computes properties.

New: tell the model when it is wrong New

Every prediction now carries a rating control. You can mark it good or bad, or submit the fragment you believe is correct. Nothing is stored unless you both rate a prediction and opt in to sharing — see §11.

AI model

Drug Classification /drug_class

Predicts a compound's therapeutic class directly from its structure across 12 categories: anti-infective, antineoplastic, central nervous system, cardiovascular, anti-inflammatory, hematologic, gastrointestinal, lipid-regulating, respiratory, reproductive control, dermatologic and urological. Compounds can carry more than one class.

Fluoxetine (Prozac) is classified as Central nervous system. The model's raw text output was exactly cns (similarity 1.00); runners-up are listed with their scores.

How it works

  • The same transformer architecture, fine-tuned on 8,336 labelled compounds, writes the class name as text.
  • That text is matched to the 38 known labels (single classes and observed combinations) by edit distance, so every answer is a valid class even when the raw output is noisy.
  • The Closest label matches chart shows how decisive the prediction was.

Also included

  • A drug-likeness profile: Lipinski's Rule of Five, Veber rules, QED score, TPSA, H-bond donors and acceptors, rotatable bonds and more.
  • 2D structure and an interactive 3D conformer.
  • Batch mode: classify up to 100 compounds from a CSV, with a class-distribution chart and CSV export.
Read it as a hypothesis. The classifier learns statistical patterns, not mechanisms, and can be confidently wrong — ciprofloxacin, an antibiotic, is labelled antineoplastic. Rare classes such as urological (48 training compounds) are the least reliable.

Chemistry

Fragment Linking /combine

Combines two fragments into larger, lead-like molecules. SmileBERTa joins them through short amine (C–N) and ester linkers at open carbon positions, then keeps only candidates that pass Lipinski's Rule of Five and score QED > 0.3.

The example pair produces six drug-like candidates, ranked by QED (top score 0.88), each with molecular weight, cLogP and H-bond counts.

How to use it

  1. Fill slots A and B: type SMILES, or click fragments saved in your library from Fragment Prediction.
  2. Click Link fragments.
  3. Review candidates sorted by QED, open any in 3D, then save, dock, profile or refine them in the Builder.

Why the filters matter

Joining fragments easily produces molecules that are too large or too greasy to be drugs. Lipinski's rules (MW ≤ 500, cLogP ≤ 5, HBD ≤ 5, HBA ≤ 10) and QED — similarity to approved oral drugs — keep only realistic candidates, which saves time before docking.

Force field

Molecule Builder /builder

An Avogadro-style 3D molecule editor. Build molecules atom by atom with automatic hydrogens, then refine them with real molecular-mechanics force fields (MMFF94, MMFF94s, UFF) and search for low-energy conformers.

Caffeine optimised with MMFF94: the energy fell from −30.1 to −122.5 kcal/mol over 1,208 minimisation steps, animated live with the trace plotted beside it.

Building tools

  • Draw: click an atom to grow a bonded atom of the selected element. Clicking a hydrogen replaces it.
  • Bond: click two atoms to bond them, or cycle single, double and triple.
  • Delete, Charge (cycle 0 / +1 / −1), and Measure (distance, angle, dihedral).
  • Element palette (C, H, N, O, S, P, F, Cl, Br, I, B, Si), keyboard shortcuts, undo/redo, and valence errors highlighted in red.
  • Start from SMILES or templates (benzene, indole, caffeine, aspirin…), or import SDF/MOL/XYZ/PDB.

Physics

  • Auto-clean: a fast in-browser spring model tidies geometry after every edit.
  • Optimise geometry: server-side RDKit minimisation (BFGS) with MMFF94, MMFF94s or UFF, recording frames that animate in the viewer.
  • Conformer search: ETKDG v3 generates diverse shapes, MMFF94 minimises each, and duplicates are removed and ranked by relative energy.
  • Export SDF, XYZ, PDB, SMILES or PNG, save to the workspace, or send straight to Docking.

Physics · rebuilt this release

Docking with AutoDock Vina /docking

Predicts how a ligand binds a protein and estimates binding affinity using AutoDock Vina 1.2.7, one of the most widely used docking engines. Load any structure from the Protein Data Bank, define the pocket, dock, and inspect each pose with its hydrogen bonds and contact residues.

Roughly fifty times faster than the first release Rebuilt

Docking used to share a free-tier server with the machine-learning models, on about a tenth of one CPU core, running one job at a time at the lowest scheduling priority. A drug-sized run took around half an hour, which reads as broken. It now runs on a dedicated two-core server of its own, with a real job queue.

Measured on the live siteBeforeAfter
Benzamidine → trypsin (PDB 3PTB)38 s3.7 s
Imatinib → EGFR~30 min35 s

Four steps

  1. Receptor: fetch a PDB ID (HIV-1 protease, SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, EGFR, acetylcholinesterase, trypsin…) or upload a file. Choose chains.
  2. Ligand: SMILES, an example drug, or a 3D model sent from the Builder.
  3. Search box: snap to a co-crystallised ligand, pick a point on the structure, or type coordinates. The box is drawn live as a wireframe so the protein stays visible.
  4. Run: set exhaustiveness and number of poses, then follow live progress and the log.

Pipeline & results

  • Receptor cleaned, polar hydrogens added at pH 7.4, AutoDock atom types assigned (Open Babel).
  • Ligand embedded in 3D (ETKDG + MMFF94) and converted to a flexible torsion tree.
  • Vina's empirical scoring function searches with Monte-Carlo + BFGS.
  • Results: pose table (kcal/mol, RMSD), estimated Ki, H-bonds ≤ 3.5 Å, residues within 4 Å, and downloads as SDF, PDBQT, complex PDB or CSV.
Fair-use limits. Free docking allows two jobs at a time and forty an hour — enough for an afternoon of real work. Limits are counted per browser rather than per network, so a whole classroom on one campus connection does not share a single quota.

Visualization

Structure Studio /studio

A Chimera-style structure explorer for proteins, nucleic acids and small molecules. Look at individual chains, the backbone, side chains, ligands, pockets and surfaces, measure geometry, and save the full view as a session.

Hemoglobin (PDB 4HHB) coloured by secondary structure, with side chains and heme groups shown; the panel lists all four chains and their cofactors.

Representations

Cartoon, tube, backbone atoms, lines, sticks, ball-and-stick, spheres. Optional side chains, ligands, waters and hydrogens.

Colouring & surfaces

By chain, secondary structure, rainbow N→C, element, residue type, Kyte–Doolittle hydrophobicity, B-factor or one colour. Solvent-excluded, solvent-accessible or van der Waals surfaces.

Chains

Every chain listed with residue and atom counts. Show, hide, zoom to or isolate any chain, and jump to any ligand or cofactor.

Selections & pockets

Binding site: residues within 4–8 Å of any ligand, labelled. Custom queries by chain, residue range, residue name, atom name or element, added as styled layers.

Measurements

Click atoms to measure distance, angle or dihedral. Clicking an atom lists its residue, B-factor and every bond with its length.

Info & sequence

Title, method, resolution and secondary-structure composition, plus a clickable sequence strip marking helices and sheets. Export PNG or save the session.

New in this release

ADMET Profiler /admet

A molecule that binds is not yet a drug. The ADMET Profiler asks the next question — would this compound be absorbed, dissolve, reach its target and avoid obvious trouble? It reports absorption and permeability, aqueous solubility, drug-likeness filters, structural alerts and synthetic accessibility for one molecule in about six milliseconds, or for up to 200 in a batch.

Every number carries the equation, rule or paper it came from. Nothing is invented.

What it reports

  • Absorption: the BOILED-Egg model for passive gut absorption and blood–brain-barrier access, plus TPSA and descriptor ranges.
  • Solubility: ESOL log S, converted to mg/mL and a solubility class.
  • Drug-likeness: Lipinski, Veber, Egan, Muegge, Ghose and QED, each with the exact rule that passed or failed.
  • Alerts: PAINS and Brenk substructure catalogues, with the matching fragment named.
  • Make-ability: synthetic accessibility and natural-product likeness scores.

The design rule

Each value carries a basis — equation, rule, range, substructure or descriptor — and a citation. Endpoints that genuinely need a trained model, such as CYP inhibition, P-gp substrate status, hERG, Ames and LD50, are returned in an explicit unavailable list that points to the Model Hub, rather than being filled in with a plausible number. A profile you cannot check is not useful to a researcher.

Built on RDKit and published models, so it adds no heavy dependencies and runs alongside the language models without competing for memory.

A correction found while building it. Widely copied secondary summaries state the BOILED-Egg brain-access box as -2.9 < TPSA < 79.1, which is impossible — TPSA cannot be negative, and the two axes have been transposed somewhere along the way. Checked against twenty known drugs, the corrected orientation identifies 10 of 10 CNS drugs while the literal one misses caffeine. SmileBERTa uses the corrected reading and says so.

Model Hub /models

SmileBERTa's own models answer two questions well. The Model Hub is the honest admission that there are hundreds of others — and a way to use them. Browse chemistry and biology models on Hugging Face, read what each one does, then generate a ready-to-run notebook that loads the model, prepares your data and produces results.

NotebookWhat the generated code does
PredictRun predictions on your own molecules with a pretrained model.
EmbedTurn molecules into vectors and train a baseline model on top of them.
Fine-tuneContinue training the model on your own labelled data.
DescriptorsCompute RDKit descriptors and drug-likeness for a dataset.

Why this belongs here

The gap between reading about a model and running one is mostly plumbing: the right library versions, a tokenizer, a dataframe in the right shape. A generated notebook removes that afternoon of work, and it runs on free Colab hardware rather than on SmileBERTa's servers.

Where the unavailable endpoints go

When the ADMET Profiler declines to guess at hERG or CYP inhibition, it links here. The platform's answer to "we cannot compute that honestly" is to hand you the tools to compute it yourself, not to approximate it.

Accounts, Workspace & feedback

Your work, kept

Every tool works without signing in. A free account adds a personal Workspace where molecules, predictions, docking runs, linked leads and structure sessions are saved permanently and can be reopened exactly as they were.

Workspace

  • Save from any tool; Open returns to that tool with the saved state restored — docking poses, studio views, built molecules.
  • The fragment library is synced to the account and shared by Fragment Prediction and Linking.
  • Counts by type, filters and search; rename, export as JSON, delete.
  • Change password, or delete the account and everything in it.

Admin

Accounts named in the server's ADMIN_USERNAMES setting get a dashboard of all users, saved items by type, recent activity and server health.

Security & reliability

  • Passwords stored only as salted hashes (Werkzeug PBKDF2), never in plain text.
  • Sign-in uses random session tokens that expire after 90 days; signing out revokes them.
  • Data lives in a managed Neon Postgres database, so it survives restarts and redeploys.
  • If the database is briefly unreachable, users see a "try again" message and no data is lost.
  • Sign-in retries automatically when a connection stalls, instead of waiting out a long timeout.

Model feedback — opt-in, and off by default New

Predictions carry a rating control: good, bad, or "here is the fragment I would have expected". This is how the model gets better over time (§16).

  • Nothing is stored unless you both rate a prediction and opt in to sharing.
  • The consent dialog states exactly what is kept, and warns against enabling it for unpublished structures.
  • A Workspace toggle turns it off again at any time.
  • The terms of service and privacy policy describe this in plain language.

Credits & policies

Everything borrowed is named New

The /credits page lists every tool, model, data source and published method the platform uses, each with its citation, DOI and licence — copyable as a block or exportable as a text file. A researcher can reproduce or cite anything SmileBERTa computed for them.

Licences were read from installed package metadata rather than recalled. One dependency in the core prediction path carried a copyleft licence and was replaced with a permissive equivalent, verified identical on 20,000 random pairs before the swap shipped.

A terms of service and privacy policy now cover what the platform stores, what feedback is used for, and the limits of what predictions mean.

For research and education only. Not for clinical use. Model predictions and docking scores are hypotheses for prioritising experiments, never substitutes for laboratory or clinical evidence.

Engineering decisions worth naming

  • Memory-aware model cache. Each model needs about 250 MB, so on a 512 MB tier one stays resident and the other loads on demand. Int8 quantisation was rejected: it changed 15% of fragment predictions.
  • Docking on its own hardware. Vina is CPU-bound and parallelises well, so the deciding factor was cores per dollar. A dedicated host beat the equivalent managed tier by roughly ten times on price.
  • Resilient database layer. A fresh connection per request with retries, and no silent fallback to temporary storage — a database outage returns an error rather than quietly losing work.
  • Rate limits per browser, not per network. A classroom behind one campus IP would otherwise share a single quota.

The vision

Where this is going

Computational drug discovery should be something a curious student can do on a Tuesday afternoon — and something a working lab can trust on a Wednesday.

Today SmileBERTa is a workbench: eight tools that answer eight questions and hand results to each other. The ambition is for it to become something a workbench cannot be on its own — a system that gets measurably better the more it is used, because it can check its own predictions.

The loop that closes

Most chemistry models are trained once and shipped. SmileBERTa is unusual in doing fragment prediction and physics-based docking in the same place. That means a prediction can be scored without waiting for a human to label it: a predicted fragment that docks well into the intended pocket is evidence the prediction was good, generated automatically.

Predict
model proposes a fragment
→
Dock
physics scores it
→
Measure
benchmark, every round
→
Retrain
offline, in curated batches

Explicit user ratings (§11) are the first, smallest version of this signal. The automatic version — using docking outcomes as labels — is the one that matters, and it is not built yet.

Said plainly, because it is easy to overclaim. The model does not learn from conversations as they happen. Online learning on live user input drifts, degrades and can be poisoned. The right shape is to collect continuously, retrain deliberately offline, and measure every round — which is why a published benchmark comes before any retraining, and why "it improves with use" is not something to sell until it demonstrably has.

16 · OutlookSmileBERTa · Platform Overview

Who it is for, in order

Students first

A student with no software, no cluster and no budget can classify a drug, pull it apart, rebuild it, dock it and profile it in an afternoon — and read why each step works. This is the audience the platform is already good for.

Classrooms next

The same workflow, with class codes, an instructor view and limits sized for thirty students at once. The rate-limiting and queueing work is already pointed at this.

Then working labs

Groups that need fast triage — which of these forty compounds deserves a synthesis slot — with results that survive restarts, an API, and a person to ask when something looks wrong.

How it pays for itself

The core stays free, permanently and without an account. Published pricing names two planned tiers — Researcher at $5/month for people who run this daily and need it not to queue, and Lab at $20/month for a group sharing one target and one dataset. Nothing is charged today, and the pricing page says so in those words.

That is deliberately cheap, and it is affordable because of an engineering choice: docking runs on hardware picked for cores per dollar rather than convenience, which is what made a thirty-minute job into a thirty-five-second one at a cost of a few euros a month. Low prices are a consequence of the architecture, not a discount.

What would make this fail

  • Claiming more than the numbers support. A researcher forgives a limitation that is documented; they do not come back after a prediction that was quietly wrong. Every unavailable endpoint, every known misclassification and every docking caveat in this document is there for that reason.
  • Feedback without measurement. A few hundred ratings from twenty beta users, mostly on aspirin and imatinib, will not move a transformer. Treated as a six-to-twelve-month asset it is valuable; treated as a launch feature it is a story.
  • Growing surface area instead of depth. A ninth tool is easier to build than a real benchmark for the two models that already exist. The benchmark is the harder and more valuable thing.
The next real milestone is not a feature. It is a research group using SmileBERTa on their own compounds, and saying whether it helped.

Limitations & roadmap

What it cannot do yet

For research and education only, not for clinical use. Model predictions and docking scores are hypotheses to prioritise experiments, not substitutes for laboratory or clinical evidence.
LimitationWhat it means in practice
Target selector is cosmeticThe fragment tool records the chosen target (KRAS, mTOR, AKT1, ERK2), but the current checkpoint is a single shared model, so predictions do not yet change with the target.
Classifier errorsIt learns statistical patterns, not mechanisms, and can be confidently wrong. Ciprofloxacin is labelled antineoplastic; rare classes such as urological are least reliable.
Docking assumptionsThe protein is rigid, explicit waters and entropy are largely ignored, and Vina's typical error is about 2 kcal/mol. Use it to compare related compounds, and validate by redocking.
ADMET coverageCYP inhibition, P-gp substrate status, hERG, Ames and LD50 need trained models and are reported as unavailable rather than estimated.
No published benchmarkBoth models are demonstrated with real examples, not yet with held-out evaluation numbers. This is the next piece of work.
Cold startsThe free-tier API sleeps when idle, so the first request after a quiet period can take about a minute. Docking, on its own host, does not sleep.

Near term

  • Published benchmarks for both models
  • Paid hosting to remove cold starts
  • Custom domain and professional email
  • Email verification and password reset
  • A research group's beta test, and what it tells us

Growth

  • Target-specific fragment models
  • Docking outcomes as automatic training labels
  • Researcher and Lab plans; classroom licences
  • API access for large-scale screening
  • Pharmacophore search; ML-based ADMET endpoints
